What else you need, beyond the three sliders

Cars, mean speed and spread are the right place to start, but on their own they cannot produce a jam — a spread of speeds on an empty road just means people arrive at different times. A traffic jam is what happens when the interaction rules bite. These are the pieces that actually matter, and where each one sits in the simulation above.

In the sim

Density, not car count

Twenty cars over 500 m and twenty cars over 5 km are completely different worlds. Car count only means something once you fix road length and lane count, so the real input is vehicles per kilometre per lane. Free flow gives way to stop-and-go somewhere around 25–30.

Structural

A car-following rule

Without one, fast cars drive straight through slow ones. This uses the Intelligent Driver Model: each car aims for its desired speed but holds a gap of s0 + v·T, braking harder the faster it is closing. That gap term is what turns one slow car into everyone else's problem.

In the sim

Imprecision and reaction lag

Perfect drivers on a uniform road never jam — small disturbances just damp out. Add human error and a delay between seeing and reacting, and disturbances amplify instead. At the default density, zeroing both takes the flow from ragged stop-and-go to nearly uniform and lifts throughput by about a fifth. That gap is the cost of being human.

Structural

Braking beats accelerating

People brake at 3–4 m/s² but pull away at barely 1. That asymmetry is why a jam is not symmetric in time: it forms almost instantly and dissolves slowly, and why the whole structure crawls backwards along the road while every car inside it is moving forwards.

In the sim

Lane-changing rules

Three lanes without lane changes are just three separate one-lane simulations. Cars here use a MOBIL-style rule: change only if it is safe, if it improves your own acceleration, and if the cost imposed on the driver you cut in front of — weighted by courtesy — does not outweigh the gain. A keep-left bias holds lane 3 for overtaking.

In the sim

Vehicle mix

A lorry is not a slow car. It is three and a half times longer, so it eats road space; it accelerates at a third of the rate, so it holds up everything behind it leaving a jam; and because every limiter is set to about the same 90 km/h, lorries bunch into platoons and one overtaking another takes a mile. At the default density, putting 15% lorries on the road costs about twice as much throughput as winding the spread of car speeds from zero to its widest.

In the sim

Boundary conditions

A closed loop keeps the car count fixed, which is what you want for isolating spontaneous jams: nothing enters, nothing leaves, so anything that happens came from the traffic itself. An open road instead takes a demand in vehicles per hour and lets traffic leave at the far end, which is the only way to ask whether the road can carry what is being asked of it. Switch between them and the same sliders answer different questions.

In the sim

A bottleneck

Most real jams are not phantoms. They are pinned to a lane closure, an on-ramp, a gradient or a bend. Turn the closure on and the jam stops wandering and parks itself upstream of the cones. The merge is the subtler one: with a long enough slip road and drivers who move over, it costs almost nothing, but shorten it and the same traffic breaks down and stays broken.

In the sim

Anything that makes you stop

The toll plaza is the most brutal thing here, and not because paying takes long. A vehicle has to brake to a halt, transact, pull away, and let the next one roll forward and stop again — and that cycle costs about eight seconds per vehicle before any money changes hands. Set the dwell to half a second and a lane still manages only around 460 vehicles an hour, against more than 2,000 flowing. Stopping is the expensive part, which is the whole argument for free-flow tolling.

Structural

What you measure

The animation is persuasive but the time–space diagram is the evidence: it makes the backward-travelling waves visible and measurable. The fundamental diagram is the payoff — the point where more cars start delivering less throughput, and the capacity drop that means clearing a jam takes fewer cars than causing it.

One more is worth knowing about, deliberately left out here to keep the controls honest: heterogeneous headways. Tailgaters and cautious drivers differ far more in following distance than in top speed, and following distance is what sets capacity — so a spread on the headway slider would arguably do more work than the spread on desired speeds does.

Integration is ballistic at a 50 ms step, with per-vehicle parameters redrawn on reset. A last-resort braking rule stops cars overlapping when a merge goes badly; in ordinary traffic it never fires. Speeds are colour-coded against the fleet's mean desired speed, so a slow driver cruising happily still reads as free-flowing.
